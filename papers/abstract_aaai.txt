We study learning to allocate scarce treatments, such as housing assistance or hospital interventions, when a policy trained on observational data must respect long-run capacity constraints. The standard approach is two-stage: fit per-arm outcome models, then compute dual capacity prices that turn the predictions into a feasible assignment rule. Its prediction stage is decision-blind, since errors that flip allocations cost no more in training than errors that change nothing. We instead train the scoring model end-to-end, differentiating an inverse-propensity-weighted estimate of policy value through the equilibrium prices themselves, which preserves the dual-price structure that makes the policy implementable online. Two inner objectives give a choice of guarantees: a nonconvex formulation self-consistent with the deployed softmax policy, and a convex relaxation whose stationary points are exactly feasible in expectation, within a bounded optimality gap of $\tau\log|\mathcal{T}|$. We evaluate on several datasets, including real and synthetic, under a queueing simulator that prices infeasibility and delay by their deployed consequences. The two end-to-end variants take the top slots on the deployment-adjusted value index at every delay cost, including zero delay. Two-stage baselines deploy above their capacity caps and wait several times longer in queue. On the largest dataset, a hospital cohort of seventy thousand patients, end-to-end also wins raw policy value, a margin that is statistically significant and survives a capacity-matched neural baseline. Where ground truth is measurable, flexible two-stage regression keeps a raw-value lead; the contribution of end-to-end training is that its policies are deployable as trained.
